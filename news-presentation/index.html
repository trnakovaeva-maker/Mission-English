The key difference between Level 2 and Level 3
Level 2
Reports facts · basic opinions · describes the situation · answers questions about the story
Level 3
Analyses causes · defends opinions under pressure · hypothesises · connects to broader context
Common mistakes to avoid
✕
Staying on the personal/concrete level — describing only your own experience instead of the broader issue
✕
Avoiding complex grammar out of fear — this keeps you at Level 2
✕
Not committing to an opinion — saying "it's complicated" without taking a position
✕
One-sentence responses — develop every answer; the examiner needs more to work with
1
Introduce the story
One or two sentences. You are not reading the article aloud — just give the examiner enough to follow you.
Required at Level 2 Required at Level 3
Starter phrases
Example
I'd like to talk about a story that caught my attention this week. It concerns...
Something that's been in the news recently is the question of...
The story I've chosen is about... It involves...
I came across an interesting report about... The main issue is...
Example opening
"I'd like to talk about a story that caught my attention this week. It's about the ceasefire agreed between Israel and Lebanon on 16 April, brokered by the United States."
2
Explain why you chose it
Explicitly required by the Czech exam. Your first opportunity to show Level 3 thinking — connect the story to something broader.
Level 3 opportunity
Level 2 vs Level 3
Level 2: "I chose it because it's important."
Level 3: Connect to a broader issue, a principle, or your professional context as a soldier.
Useful phrases
Example
Military angle
I found this particularly interesting because it connects directly to...
What struck me about this story is that it raises a question about...
This caught my attention because it seems to reflect a broader trend in...
I think this is significant because it has direct implications for...
From a military perspective, this matters because...
Example — Level 3 response
"I chose this because it raises a question that goes beyond this specific conflict — whether ceasefires brokered under external pressure can produce genuine, lasting peace. As a soldier, I'm also interested in what this means for peacekeeping operations like UNIFIL, which has forces on the ground in Lebanon."
As a soldier who has served on international missions, I find this relevant because...
From a NATO perspective, this is significant because...
In the context of Czech defence policy, this raises the question of...
This connects to something we deal with professionally — namely the question of...
3
Give the key facts
A few sentences covering what happened, who is involved, and what is at stake. Keep it natural — you are retelling the story, not reciting it.
Required at Level 2 Required at Level 3
Structuring phrases
Contrast & nuance
Example
The background to this is... / To give some context...
The key development is that... / According to reports...
What makes this significant is... / What is unusual here is...
This comes at a time when... / In the broader context of...
On one hand... on the other hand...
While X is true, it is also the case that...
Despite..., the reality is that...
This is complicated by the fact that...
Example
"The ceasefire was announced on 16 April and took effect the same evening, establishing a 10-day pause in fighting. It includes the first direct Israel-Lebanon peace talks since 1983. However, Hezbollah was not a formal signatory, and Israel retained the right to carry out self-defence strikes at any time."
4
Predict or analyse
The Czech exam explicitly requires this. Move beyond the facts — show you can reason about what might happen next and why.
This is where Level 3 is won or lost
Remember
The examiner is not listening for the right answer. They are listening for your ability to reason, hypothesise, and defend a view. A confident wrong prediction is better than a vague correct one.

Full Level 3 prediction
"I'm sceptical this ceasefire will lead to a lasting settlement. The fundamental problem is that it doesn't resolve the core issue — Hezbollah's weapons and its relationship to Iran. This raises a broader question about whether external pressure alone can ever produce genuine political will. The real test won't be whether the shooting stops for ten days, but whether both sides are willing to make the compromises that a permanent agreement requires."
The opinion ladder — use it on every answer
1
State your opinion
"In my view... / I think... / I believe..."
2
Give a reason or evidence
"...because... / This is supported by the fact that... / We can see this in..."
3
Go abstract or hypothetical
"More broadly... / The deeper issue here is... / If this continues... / What this ultimately comes down to is..."

Opinion questions
"What do you think about...?" / "Do you agree with...?"
most common
These are invitations to climb the ladder. Don't just say yes or no — commit to a position and develop it.
Do
State a clear position immediately
Give at least one reason
Connect to a broader point
Use "I would argue that..."
Don't
Say "it depends" without more
Describe the situation again
Give a one-sentence answer
Agree immediately to avoid conflict
In my view,... and I think this matters because...
I would argue that... The evidence for this is...
Personally, I think... though I recognise that others would say...
›
Devil's advocate questions
"Some people would argue the opposite..." / "I'm not sure I agree..."
Level 3 test
The examiner is not attacking you — they are testing whether you can defend a position under pressure. Don't collapse. Acknowledge the challenge and hold your ground.
Do
Acknowledge the counterargument briefly
Defend your position with new reasoning
Concede a specific point if it's fair
Stay confident and calm
Don't
Immediately say "yes, you're right"
Abandon your position entirely
Get flustered or go silent
Repeat your original answer unchanged
That's a fair point, although I would argue that...
I can see why some people think that, but in my view...
You're right that X, but I think the more important issue is...
I accept that point, though it doesn't change my overall view that...
›
Hypothetical questions
"What would you do if...?" / "What if the opposite had happened...?"
Level 3 marker
These questions move you away from facts into reasoning. Use conditional structures — this is where your grammar signals Level 3.
Do
Use "If I were... I would..."
Use "Were this to happen..."
Think aloud — reason through it
Give a concrete answer then broaden
Don't
Say "I don't know" and stop
Answer in the present tense only
Give only one sentence
Refuse to speculate
If I were in that position, I would probably... because...
Were that to happen, I think the most likely consequence would be...
It's difficult to say with certainty, but my sense is that...
In the worst-case scenario... / In the best-case scenario...
›
Abstraction questions
"What does this tell us more generally...?" / "Is this a wider trend...?"
Level 3 marker
The examiner is pulling you away from the specific story toward the principle behind it. Don't return to the facts — go higher.
Do
Connect to a general principle
Reference NATO, EU, Czech context
Compare to similar situations
Name the underlying tension
Don't
Repeat the specific facts again
Stay at the level of one country
Give a short answer and wait
Say "I'm not sure about that"
I think this reflects a broader pattern where...
What this story really illustrates is the tension between... and...
We've seen similar situations in... and the outcome was...
From a NATO / Czech / European perspective, this raises the question of...
